The Freelance Cash Flow Spreadsheet I Actually Use (Runway, Tax, and a 12-Month Forecast)

Most freelance spreadsheets are a list of income in one tab and a list of expenses in another, and the total at the bottom is called "profit". That number is comfortable and it is close to useless. Two things happen to a self-employed person's money that a profit figure cannot show you: the tax bill arrives six months after the income, and a client pays sixty days after the invoice.

A cash-flow spreadsheet is a different animal. It answers one question: on any given month, will money actually be in my account?

Why profit lied to me

In one month I invoiced $8,400, spent $1,900, and looked at a comfortable $6,500 surplus. That month also contained: a quarter of income tax, $2,100 of software renewals that all landed on the same day, and two clients who paid in ninety days instead of thirty. The account balance at the end of the month was $900.

Nothing was wrong with the business. The spreadsheet was just measuring the wrong thing. A profit statement measures accruals — work done, work invoiced. A bank account measures cash. For a freelancer the second one is what actually has to survive.

The five tabs

Tab 1 — Income

Columns: Date received, Client, Invoice number, Amount, Currency, Exchange rate, Amount in home currency. One row per payment received, not per invoice sent. If you invoice on the 1st and get paid on the 28th, the money arrives on the 28th and this tab records the 28th.

Amount (home currency) = Amount * Exchange rate

Keep the exchange-rate column even if you invoice in your own currency. The moment a client pays in dollars and your bank converts it, the number in your account will not match the number on the invoice, and you want to know that difference rather than discover it.

Tab 2 — Expected income (the pipeline)

Columns: Client, Invoice number, Amount, Date issued, Expected payment date, Days overdue, Status. This tab is your collection pipeline and it should be the first thing you look at on a Monday.

Days overdue = IF(Status <> "Paid", MAX(0, TODAY() - Expected payment date), 0)

The rule that saved me: anything past thirty days gets a polite email the same day it crosses the line, and anything past sixty days gets a phone call. Not to be difficult — to be professional. Money that is late is usually late because the invoice was not chased, and the chase is the thing that has to be systematic.

Tab 3 — Expenses

Columns: Date, Category (select: Software, Equipment, Tax, Insurance, Subcontractors, Marketing, Travel, Other), Description, Amount, Recurring? (checkbox), Deductible? (checkbox). The two checkboxes are what turn this tab into something useful at tax time, because by March you will not remember what the $47 charge in October was.

Tab 4 — Tax reserve

One row per month. Month, Income in month, Reserve % (put in your actual marginal rate plus a margin — 30% is a common starting point, 25% if you are genuinely below the threshold), Reserved, Balance. The formula is deliberately boring:

Reserved = Income in month * Reserve %
Balance  = previous Balance + Reserved - Tax set aside

The point of a separate tab is that the money is moved. Transfer the reserved amount to a second account the day you calculate it. Money in a spreadsheet is a note; money in a different account is a decision.

Tab 5 — 12-month forecast

This is the tab that changes behaviour, because it shows you the bad month coming. Rows are months, columns are: Expected income (from Tab 2, grouped by expected payment date), Expected expenses (from Tab 3, grouped by month and recurring flag), Tax set-aside, Net, Opening balance, Closing balance.

Closing balance = Opening balance + Expected income
                   - Expected expenses - Tax set aside

When the closing balance for month four goes negative, you have two months to fix it: send the invoice that has been sitting, push the retainer, or delay a purchase. That is a decision you can make calmly in March. Finding out in April is not a decision, it is a reaction.

Runway, invoice aging and a twelve-month forecast with live formulas — that is the Cash-Flow Command Center, built in a spreadsheet you can open today.

The three numbers to check every month

You do not need to read five tabs. Three numbers, on the first working day of the month:

  1. Closing balance, forecast. What will this month end with? If it is negative, you have work to do.
  2. Total overdue. Sum of Tab 2's days-overdue column, weighted by amount. This is the number you can change today.
  3. Tax reserve balance. What you have actually set aside against what you owe. The gap between these two is how tax season turns into a crisis.

Common mistakes

  • Recording income when it is invoiced. This is the mistake that makes a cash spreadsheet behave like a profit statement. Income enters Tab 1 on the day the money lands.
  • Forgetting quarterly tax. If you are in a regime where tax is paid quarterly, the reserve is not an annual number you work out in April — it is a monthly transfer.
  • One big table. Five tabs is not bureaucracy. Each tab has one job, and the formula in Tab 5 reads from Tabs 2 and 3. Merging them is how forecasts break.
  • Never opening it. A spreadsheet that is only correct on the day you build it is a diary, not a system. Twenty minutes on the first of the month is the whole commitment.
