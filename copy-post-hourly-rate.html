What your real hourly rate is (most freelancers never calculate it)

Nobody tells you that your hourly rate is a lie. You set it once, years ago, and you have not revisited it since. Meanwhile the work around the work — the scoping calls, the revisions, the invoicing, the chasing, the sales call that won the job — keeps growing quietly around the project.

The example that stings

You quote $4,000 for a forty-hour project and feel good about it. That is $100 an hour, right?

Here is what actually happened: six hours of scoping calls before you quoted, eleven hours of revisions, three hours of invoicing and administration, four hours chasing a payment that was late, and five hours of the sales conversation that won the job in the first place. That is twenty-nine hours of work that nobody put on the invoice. Your real rate was $34 an hour.

$4,000 divided by 117 actual hours is $34.19. Not $100.

The three numbers that matter more

Before you raise a rate, know these:

  1. Money in the bank — not what you are owed, what is actually there.
  2. Money owed to you — and how old the oldest invoice is.
  3. Money going out every month — software, subscriptions, accountant, the coffee you do not need but buy anyway because you are in the same place as your client.

Runway is the first number divided by the third. Under three months, the priority is not growth — it is collecting. Over six, you can afford to turn down the wrong project, and you should, because that is the only way the right one becomes affordable.

How to use the number

The effective rate is not a punishment. It is the input to every decision you were making by feel. When you know that the project you are about to quote has a realistic effective rate of $45 an hour, you can either quote $3,200 instead of $4,000, or change the shape of the project so the admin does not eat a fifth of it.

Both are better than guessing.
