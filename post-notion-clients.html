The Notion template I use to track freelance clients — and what I would change

SoloForge · notion template freelance client tracker, freelance client database, notion for freelancers

Most solo professionals do not need more software. They need one place where the information already exists. That is what this Notion workspace is: six linked databases, twelve views, and about thirty formulas — the boring operational layer of a client business, already built.

The six databases

Clients holds one row per person or company, with their contact details, their rate, and a relation to everything else. Projects holds the work, with a status, a start date, a deadline, and a relation to the client who pays for it. Invoices holds the money: number, amount, issue date, due date, status, and a formula for days overdue. Tasks is the list you actually look at every morning, related to both the client and the project. Time Log is the one most people skip, and it is the one that changes your pricing. Testimonials holds the words you were given after the good months, so you are not writing them from memory in a hurry.

The whole thing takes about twenty minutes to set up, and it works on the free Notion plan.

The formula that changed my rate

For six months I tracked my time against the project it belonged to, with one extra column: billable, yes or no. Scoping calls. Invoicing. Chasing a late payment. The sales call that won the job in the first place.

The number that came out of that month was roughly one hour in five. Unbillable. I had been pricing every project as if that time did not exist, which means I was giving away about a fifth of the work for free.

Two things changed. My rate went up, because the admin stopped being invisible. And I started declining projects that were mostly admin with a bit of design attached — which, looking back, were quietly the worst projects I had ever taken.

The views that matter

Twelve views sounds like a lot. It is not, because each one answers a question you ask every week:

  • Needs reply — clients with no contact in fourteen days.
  • Overdue — invoices sorted by how late they are, worst first.
  • This week — tasks with a date inside the next seven days.
  • Effective rate — money earned per hour actually worked.
  • Pipeline — quoted work, by stage, with the value attached.
  • Capacity — committed hours versus the hours you actually have.

What I would change

The Time Log is the first thing to set up, and the last thing most people get round to. Start there. The formulas are all written and explained in the setup guide, so you are copying working expressions rather than writing them.
