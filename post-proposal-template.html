How to write a freelance proposal that gets accepted (template included)

SoloForge · freelance proposal template, how to write a proposal, freelance proposal examples

Most freelance proposals do not get rejected because the price was too high. They get rejected because the client could not tell what they were getting, or because the scope was vague enough that they were afraid of what it might turn into.

Two pages, three sections

A proposal that works fits on two pages. If it does not, the extra length is usually hiding a decision you have not made yet.

1. What you asked for (in their words)
Repeat their brief back to them, using their vocabulary, not yours. This section is not decoration — it is how you find out that you understood the brief differently. If you cannot write their brief in their words, you are not ready to quote.

2. What I propose, and what is not included
Deliverables, in numbered order, each specific enough to be recognised as done. Then the section most people skip:

Not included: ongoing maintenance, additional pages beyond the agreed number, third-party licences, and hosting costs. Any of these can be added — just say so in the reply and I will price them separately.

That paragraph is the most valuable thing in the document. It saves the argument you would otherwise have in month three, and it protects the margin you priced in month zero.

3. Timeline, price, one next step
Dates, not phases. Price with what it includes and what it excludes. One next step, and only one: "Reply here and I will start on Monday." Never two questions at the end of a proposal — two questions means neither gets answered.

The questions that make quoting fast

Three questions turn a vague brief into something you can price:

  1. What does "done" look like, in a sentence the client would recognise?
  2. What is explicitly not included?
  3. Who approves the work, and how long does that usually take?

Most "the client went quiet" problems are actually question three. Someone senior has to say yes, and nobody told you that — so you chased the wrong person for three weeks.

Write it once, then edit it into your voice

The fastest way to send a good proposal is not to think of it each time. It is to keep a set of prompts for the four documents solo professionals actually write — the proposal, the scope, the invoice cover, and the follow-up — and fill in the blanks.
