How to Raise Your Freelance Rate Without Losing Clients (The Numbers to Bring)

SoloForge · how to raise freelance rate, increase my freelance rate, freelance rate increase email

Almost every freelance rate increase goes wrong in the same way: the freelancer decides on a number in isolation, sends a slightly awkward email, and treats a "not right now" as a verdict on their worth. Then they stay at the old rate for two more years and quietly resent the work.

A rate increase is an arithmetic problem wearing an emotional costume. Do the arithmetic first, and the email writes itself.

Step 1 — Find your real utilisation rate

Before you choose a new number, find out what you are actually being paid per hour of working time — not per hour of billable time. This is the single most useful number in freelancing and almost nobody calculates it.

Real hourly rate = (Revenue - non-billable costs) / Total working hours

Use one month of real data. Count every working hour, including admin, sales calls, invoicing, learning, and the time you spend looking for the next job. Then divide your net revenue by that number.

Here is a realistic example. Someone bills $7,400 in a month across 32 billable hours. But the month contained 48 hours of total work, 11 of which were unbillable — proposals, invoicing, a half-day of "visibility" work that was not billable, and an afternoon chasing an unpaid invoice. The nominal rate is $231/hour. The real rate is $154/hour.

If your real rate is below what you would accept as an employee, the problem is not your rate. It is the eleven hours. The time-tracking experiment shows you which ones to kill.

Most people who do this calculation for the first time find their real rate is 25–40% below their nominal rate. That gap is the single largest source of unrecognised money in a one-person business.

Step 2 — Fix the eleven hours before you raise the rate

Raising your rate on a business with bad utilisation is a trap: you raise the price, work the same hours, and get a raise that does not cover the extra complexity. Get the hours down first, then raise the rate on a business that already runs well.

The three changes that usually move the needle fastest:

  • Stop writing full proposals for weak leads. A two-page proposal for someone who has not had a call is not a sales tool, it is a discount.
  • Automate invoicing and chasing. Every hour spent chasing is an hour you are not billing, and it is also a signal to the client about how you are run.
  • Move fixed costs down. If three tools do the same job, one of them is a habit rather than a need.

Step 3 — Choose the new rate from a position, not a round number

Raise by 20–30%, not by 5%. A small increase costs you the same relationship risk as a large one — the client decides whether you are worth their budget, and if you are not, 5% will not save it. A 25% increase, delivered calmly, is read as a confident professional recalibration. A 5% increase is read as a request.

Then round to a clean number. $95/hour reads as a decision; $97.50 reads as an apology.

Step 4 — Change the scope, not just the number

The strongest version of this email does not say "my rate is now higher". It says the offer is different. Keep the price, change what is inside it — fewer revisions, a defined turnaround, a monthly cap on hours, a deliverable list instead of a vague scope. The client is buying a predictable outcome, and predictability is worth paying for.

This is also where the proposal work pays off. A scope you have written clearly is a scope the client can approve quickly, and a fast approval is the cheapest rate increase available.

Step 5 — The email

Short, factual, no apology. Here is the shape:

Hi [name],

I am updating my day rate from $[old] to $[new] for projects starting from [date].

The work I do has not changed — but what is around it has: I have taken on [specific thing, e.g. two retainer clients] and I am now booking [months] out. The scope, turnaround and deliverables stay exactly as they are.

For work already in flight, nothing changes. For anything starting from [date], it will be $[new].

Happy to walk through it on a call if that helps.

[name]

Three rules for this email: no apology, no justification paragraph, and a date. "I am raising my rate because my work has improved" invites a debate about the work. "My rate is $[new] from [date]" is a statement of fact that the client can accept or decline without either of you losing face.

Step 6 — When they say no

Some clients will say no. That is not a rejection of you; it is a statement about their budget, and it usually has nothing to do with the number.

  • Offer a smaller scope at the old rate. Half the deliverable at the old price keeps the relationship and gives you a clean reference for the next client.
  • Offer a retainer. Predictable monthly income is worth a lower hourly rate to a client who dangles on their cash flow.
  • Ask for a date. "Would [quarter] work?" converts a no into a scheduled yes. About a third of the noes turn into a yes with a date.
  • Let it go politely. A client who fights a 25% increase will not be a good client in year two. Say "totally understood, thanks for telling me" and mean it.

What to do if nobody says yes

If two rounds of increases produce zero acceptances, the problem is almost never the number — it is positioning. Clients buy the outcome they can describe to their own boss. That is what proposals, scopes and case studies are for, and it is why the Proposal & Copy Vault exists: 150+ prompts and 25 send-ready templates for the four documents that decide whether you get paid on time.

A concrete 30-day plan

  1. Week 1 — log every working hour. Calculate your real hourly rate.
  2. Week 2 — cut two of the eleven hours. Add a weekly review so it stays cut.
  3. Week 3 — write the new scope, the new price, and the email.
  4. Week 4 — send it to the clients you want to keep working with. Count acceptances, not replies.
